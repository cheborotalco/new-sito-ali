EU AI Act in practice: what businesses actually need to check before scaling AI

Most companies hear about the EU AI Act either as an abstract legal threat or as a checklist of generic policies. In reality, the regulation affects architecture, vendor contracts, and operational ownership long before legal review.

1. Start with use-case classification, not model hype

The EU AI Act regulates how an AI system is used in a specific business context, not just which model powers it. A language model summarizing internal meeting notes carries a completely different risk profile from the same model screening job candidates, scoring creditworthiness, or assisting clinical triage.

Before signing a vendor contract or deploying an internal tool, leadership needs a clear inventory of every AI-assisted workflow and its risk tier: prohibited practices, high-risk systems, limited-risk transparency obligations, or minimal-risk tools.

Key rule of thumb If an AI output directly influences access to employment, credit, essential services, healthcare, or biometric identification, treat it as high-risk until proven otherwise.

2. Separate provider obligations from deployer responsibilities

Companies often assume that buying a SaaS tool from a large vendor transfers all compliance responsibility to that vendor. Under the EU AI Act, organizations deploying AI systems in their own operations carry distinct obligations:

  • Assigning clear human oversight so staff can actually understand, challenge, and override model outputs.
  • Maintaining usage logs, input data governance, and incident escalation paths.
  • Informing affected users or employees when AI is used in decisions or interactions that concern them.
  • Verifying that vendor documentation actually covers the intended European deployment scenario.

3. Audit vendor claims before committing budget

During pre-launch reviews, the most common gap is a mismatch between what a vendor's sales deck promises ("fully EU AI Act compliant") and what their technical documentation supports. Checking data residency, logging capabilities, human-in-the-loop controls, and fallback behavior early prevents expensive rework after launch.

4. What a pragmatic readiness check looks like

You do not need a 200-page bureaucratic binder to start. A focused readiness review maps active and planned AI use cases, flags high-risk dependencies, defines ownership between business, IT, and legal, and gives leadership a concrete sequence of actions before scaling.
